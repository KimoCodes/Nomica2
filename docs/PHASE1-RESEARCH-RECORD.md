## A. Resistance-training progression, loading and exercise order

### R1 — ACSM Position Stand: Progression Models in Resistance Training for Healthy Adults
- **Publisher:** American College of Sports Medicine · *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* 2009;41(3):687–708
- **URL/DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670 · PMID 19204579
- **Verified findings:**
  - Novice trainees: loads corresponding to an 8–12 RM range.
  - Intermediate/advanced: 1–12 RM used periodically, with eventual emphasis on 1–6 RM and 3–5 min rest for strength emphasis.
  - **Increase load 2–10% when the athlete can exceed the target reps by 1–2 reps.**
  - Hypertrophy emphasis: 6–12 RM, 1–2 min rest, higher-volume multiple-set programmes.
  - Local muscular endurance: 40–60% 1RM, >15 reps, rest <90 s.
  - **Frequency: novice 2–3 d/wk, intermediate 3–4 d/wk, advanced 4–5 d/wk.**
  - **Exercise order: large muscle group before small, multi-joint before single-joint, higher intensity before lower intensity.**
- **Programming decisions it licenses:**
  - The rep ranges and rest intervals written into every NomiTips resistance session.
  - The rule that only **one** primary variable advances per week per exercise (never sets, reps and load simultaneously).
  - The frequency ceiling: a 5 d/wk full-body schedule sits at the top of the intermediate range, so recovery design is mandatory rather than optional.

### R7 — Simão, R. et al. (2012) *Exercise order in resistance training*
- **Publisher:** PubMed-indexed review · **URL/DOI:** PMID 22292516
- **Verified findings:** total repetitions and therefore session volume are greater when an exercise is placed earlier in the session, independent of muscle mass involved; chronic strength gains are greatest for exercises performed first and inhibited for those performed last; order should follow the **priority of the training goal**.
- **Programming decisions it licenses:** the fixed intra-session ordering used in every blueprint — warm-up → priority compound → secondary compound → accessory → isolation → core/carry → cooldown.

## B. Weekly aerobic and strength dose (health baseline)

### R2 — ACSM Position Stand: Quantity and Quality of Exercise for Developing and Maintaining Cardiorespiratory, Musculoskeletal, and Neuromotor Fitness in Apparently Healthy Adults
- **Publisher:** ACSM · *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 2011;43(7):1334–1359
- **URL/DOI:** PMID 21694556 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21694556/
- **Verified findings:** ≥150 min/wk moderate-intensity cardio **or** ≥75 min/wk vigorous, or an equivalent combination; **resistance exercise for each major muscle group on 2–3 d/wk**; flexibility work for each major muscle-tendon group (~60 s per exercise) on ≥2 d/wk; programmes should be modified to habitual activity, function, health status and goals.
- **Programming decisions it licenses:**
  - The weekly aerobic targets quoted in the educational content of the cardio programs.
  - The justification for scheduling explicit rest days rather than training every day.
  - The requirement that flexibility work appear at least twice per week — satisfied here by the cooldown block in every session.

### R3 — WHO Guidelines on Physical Activity and Sedentary Behaviour (2020)
- **Publisher:** World Health Organization · ISBN 978-92-4-001512-8
- **URL:** https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240015128
- **Verified findings:** adults should accumulate **150–300 min/wk** moderate-intensity aerobic activity (or 75–150 min vigorous, or an equivalent combination) and perform **muscle-strengthening activities at moderate or greater intensity involving all major muscle groups on ≥2 days/wk**.
- **Programming decisions it licenses:** the general-education framing — every challenge is positioned as a structured step *toward* the weekly guideline, never as a substitute for it, and never with a promise of a specific body outcome.

## C. Warm-up and cool-down

### R5 — McGowan, C.J., Pyne, D.B., Thompson, K.G., Rattray, B. (2015) *Warm-Up Strategies for Sport and Exercise: Mechanisms and Applications*
- **Publisher:** *Sports Medicine* 45(11):1523–1546
- **URL/DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0376-x
- **Verified findings:** warming up raises muscle temperature, increases ATP turnover and cross-bridge cycling rate, speeds VO₂ kinetics and produces post-activation potentiation; an effective active warm-up uses an aerobic portion **shorter than 15 min** followed by 4–5 activation or race-pace efforts; effects degrade if the transition to the main session is long.
- **Programming decisions it licenses:** the three-part warm-up block (pulse raiser → dynamic mobility → movement-specific activation) placed at the start of **every** session in every program, capped so the whole warm-up stays under ~6 minutes and flows straight into the first working set.

### R13 — NSCA — *Introduction to Dynamic Warm-Up* (NSCA's Guide to Program Design series)
- **Publisher:** National Strength and Conditioning Association · 2017
- **URL:** https://www.nsca.com/education/articles/kinetic-select/introduction-to-dynamic-warm-up/
- **Verified findings:** a traditional warm-up has a general component of **5–10 minutes** of low-to-moderate activity followed by a specific component of movement-pattern work; dynamic movement is preferred before training.
- **Programming decisions it licenses:** the minimum warm-up duration stated in the session template, and the rule that static stretching is placed in the cooldown rather than before the working sets.

## D. Interval / cardio conditioning

### R4 — Tabata, I. et al. (1996) *Effects of moderate-intensity endurance and high-intensity intermittent training on anaerobic capacity and VO₂max*
- **Publisher:** *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* 28(10):1327–1330
- **URL/DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1097/00005768-199610000-00018 · PMID 8897392
- **Verified findings:** the high-intensity group performed **7–8 sets of 20 s work / 10 s rest at ~170% VO₂max, 5 d/wk for 6 weeks**; VO₂max rose 7 ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹ and anaerobic capacity rose 28%. The comparison group trained at 70% VO₂max for 60 min, 5 d/wk.
- **Programming decisions it licenses:**
  - The only defensible basis for calling anything "Tabata": genuine **20:10** work-to-rest intervals.
  - Because the original protocol is supramaximal and was tested on trained subjects, a beginner-facing challenge must **scale intensity, not the 20:10 ratio** — and must say so plainly in the safety text rather than implying the original protocol is being delivered.

## E. Elastic / resistance-band training

### R6 — Lopes, J.S.S. et al. (2019) *Effects of training with elastic resistance versus conventional resistance on muscular strength: a systematic review and meta-analysis*
- **Publisher:** SAGE Open Medicine · **URL/DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1177/2050312119831116
- **Verified findings:** across 8 studies and 224 participants aged 15–88, elastic resistance produced strength gains **statistically indistinguishable** from machines and dumbbells (upper limb SMD −0.11, 95% CI −0.40 to 0.19, p=0.48; lower limb SMD 0.09, 95% CI −0.18 to 0.35, p=0.52). The authors note that **load control with elastic devices is the main practical difficulty**, and that studies typically prescribe intensity by perceived effort or by progressing band resistance.
- **Programming decisions it licenses:**
  - Treating a band-only program as a genuine strength stimulus rather than a "beginner placeholder".
  - The band progression model used in both band programs: advance by **band resistance first, then reps, then sets** — and prescribe intensity by effort/RPE in the `notes` field because a percentage of 1RM does not exist for elastic resistance.

## F. Core / trunk training

### R8 — NSCA — *Implementing Core Training Concepts into Strength Training for Sport*
- **Publisher:** National Strength and Conditioning Association · *NSCA Coach* 7(1)
- **URL:** https://www.nsca.com/contentassets/1066cf7e2c9d4e61b3c1a6e3fea988e0/coach-7.1.2-implementing-core-training-concepts-into-strength-training-for-sport.pdf
- **Verified findings:** effective core programming uses **open- and closed-chain** exercises, works in **multiple planes of motion**, uses **loaded and unloaded** conditions (including loaded carries), and mixes **stable and unstable** positions; a variety of movement and stability patterns is required rather than one repeated exercise.
- **Programming decisions it licenses:** the four-category exercise taxonomy used by the core challenge (anti-extension / anti-rotation / lateral flexion / dynamic flexion-extension), the introduction of carries, and the rotation of emphasis across the week so no two consecutive sessions target the trunk in the same way.

### R9 — Short, S. et al. (2025) *A Critical Review of Trunk and Hip Exercise Prescription: Applying Evidence for a Modern Approach*
- **Publisher:** *International Journal of Sports Physical Therapy*
- **URL:** https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/129972-a-critical-review-of-trunk-and-hip-exercise-prescription-applying-evidence-for-a-modern-approach
- **Verified findings:** the benefits of targeted trunk exercise have often been overstated; **combined methods — isolated trunk work plus progressive multi-joint movements — outperform targeted traditional exercise alone**; isolated exercise produces greater local strength gains while endurance-oriented work produces greater endurance gains; heavy compound lifts such as the squat recruit the abdominals at least as much as planking while adding whole-body co-contraction.
- **Programming decisions it licenses:** the core challenge is explicitly **not** a crunch-only program — direct trunk work is paired with compound movements every session, and the stated learning outcome is trunk control under load, not a promised aesthetic change.

### R10 — Rodríguez-Perea, Á. et al. (2023) *Core training and performance: a systematic review with meta-analysis*
- **Publisher:** indexed systematic review · **URL/DOI:** PMC10588579
- **Verified findings:** 22 studies included; core training improved balance (ES 1.17, p<0.0001), vertical jump (ES 0.69, p=0.0003) and horizontal jump (ES 0.84, p=0.01); published programmes most commonly run **6–8 weeks with 2–3 sessions per week**, with a minimum intervention length of 4 weeks.
- **Programming decisions it licenses:**
  - Honest expectation-setting: a 3-week challenge builds **motor control and local endurance**, and the program text says so instead of implying a body-composition result.
  - Awareness that 5 core sessions per week exceeds the 2–3/wk used in the evidence base — therefore each session is deliberately **short and low-volume**, and the emphasis alternates so the trunk is not loaded identically five days running.

## G. Recovery and deloads

### R11 — Bell, L., Strafford, B.W., Coleman, M., Korakakis, P.A., Nolan, D. (2023) *Integrating Deloading into Strength and Physique Sports Training Programmes: An International Delphi Consensus Approach*
- **Publisher:** indexed consensus study · **URL/DOI:** PMC10511399
- **Verified findings:** consensus definition — "deloading is a period of reduced training stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue, promote recovery, and enhance preparedness for subsequent training"; deloads are typically **~7 days every 4–6 weeks**; volume is reduced by cutting reps per set, sets per session, or both; frequency is usually left unchanged; a deload may sit in the first, middle or final week of a mesocycle depending on schedule.
- **Programming decisions it licenses:** a planned recovery week in **every program of 6 weeks or longer**, implemented by cutting sets and effort while holding frequency and exercise identity constant so the customer does not feel the program has been emptied out.

### R12 — Bell, L., Darragh, I.A.J., Travis, S.K., Rogerson, D., Nolan, D.A. (2025) *A Practical Approach to Deloading: Recommendations and Considerations for Strength and Physique Sports*
- **Publisher:** *Strength and Conditioning Journal* · **URL/DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1519/SSC.0000000000000910
- **Verified findings:** deloads are commonly pre-planned **every 4–8 weeks**; the variables adjusted are intensity of effort, training volume, session duration or frequency; deloads may also be applied reactively when fatigue or performance stalls.
- **Programming decisions it licenses:** the reactive rule written into the educational content — if performance drops for two consecutive sessions or soreness persists, the customer is told to repeat the previous week rather than push forward.

## Sources considered and deliberately not used

- No supplement, diet-plan or body-transformation literature was consulted or will be cited — those topics are outside the permitted educational scope for this catalog.
- No injury-rehabilitation or clinical sources were used. The programs are written for apparently healthy adults (R2 applies this caveat directly), and safety text will direct anyone with pain or a medical condition to a professional rather than to the program.
- No source was included on the basis of a title alone; every DOI, PMID or publisher URL above was opened and read during this phase.

## Open evidence limitations to disclose in program copy

1. **R6** shows bands match dumbbells for strength *on average*, but also that **load cannot be precisely prescribed with bands** — so band programs prescribe effort, not load.
2. **R10** rests on studies of 4+ weeks; the two 21-day challenges are shorter than the evidence they borrow from, and the copy must not overstate what 15–21 days achieves.
3. **R4** was conducted on seven trained male subjects on a cycle ergometer at supramaximal intensity; a beginner class version of "Tabata" is a **scaled derivative**, and the program text will say exactly that.
4. **R1** gives frequency ranges by training status, not by sex or by body part, and does not endorse any particular aesthetic outcome.
